Between the flags

357 people drowned in Australia in the year to June 2025. This page looks at where, when and who, and at the volunteers who pull thousands more out of the surf every summer.

By Naman, FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2. 25 October 2026.

Where Australians drown

Counting deaths alone makes big states look the most dangerous. Dividing by population gives a fairer comparison: the number of drowning deaths for every 100,000 people living in each state.

1.65

drowning deaths per 100,000 people in Western Australia, the highest state rate in 2024/25

Western Australia, Queensland, the Northern Territory and New South Wales all sit above 1.5. Victoria's rate is less than half of these.

The ACT is too small to see at this scale. It recorded one drowning death in 2024/25.

Is this year unusual?

Each line joins a state's 10-year average rate to its 2024/25 rate. Red lines went up. Five states were above their usual level, and South Australia's death count was 71% above its 10-year average.

The Northern Territory fell sharply, from 2.82 to 1.53. With only four deaths, small changes move its rate a lot.

Compared with a normal year

This map gives every state the same-sized square, so the ACT and Tasmania are as easy to see as Western Australia. Colour shows how far 2024/25 deaths were from each state's 10-year average.

South Australia had 71% more deaths than usual. Queensland and Western Australia were both 36% higher.

The blue states recorded very few deaths (four in the NT and one in the ACT), so a small change looks large.

The highest risk is far from the cities

The three regions with the highest drowning rates are all in remote northern Australia: Torres and Cape, the Top End and the Kimberley.

Among cities, Darwin's rate of 2.38 is almost double the next highest. Sydney's rate is 1.13.

These rates are based on where people lived, not where they drowned. Hollow dots are based on fewer than 10 deaths, so treat them with caution.

When and how the coast turns deadly

Coastal drowning follows the calendar. The charts below combine five years of coastal deaths across Australia, from July 2020 to June 2025.

41%

of coastal drowning deaths happen in December, January and February

Summer brings the crowds, the school holidays and the busiest patrol season. Deaths stay high into March and April, after the summer holidays end but while the water is still warm.

Most were just going for a swim

More than a third of people who drowned on the coast were swimming or playing in the water. Boating and diving follow well behind.

One in twenty died trying to rescue someone else.

Who drowns is changing

The picture of drowning as a young person's risk is out of date. Older Australians now make up a growing share of deaths.

10%

of all drowning deaths in 2024/25 were people aged 65 to 69, up from 7% on average

Grey dots show each age group's usual share over the past 10 years. Red dots show 2024/25. Pink bars mean an age group's share grew.

The biggest increases were among people aged 60 and over. Young children's share fell, from 7% to 4%.

Many were born overseas

Each line runs from a continent to Australia. Thicker lines mean more deaths among people born there.

Only 41% of people who drowned on the coast were born in Australia. Almost one in five was born in Asia, and the birthplace of another 22% is unknown.

This suggests beach safety messages need to reach people who did not grow up near Australian surf beaches.

New South Wales, up close

NSW has the most drowning deaths of any state. Surf Life Saving data lets us look along its coastline.

52

people drowned on the NSW coast in 2024/25, above the 10-year average of 46

The year before was the worst in a decade, with 61 deaths.

Every one of the 52 deaths in 2024/25 happened at an unpatrolled location, outside patrol hours or away from the red and yellow flags.

Who drowned on the NSW coast in 2024/25

Each grid shows 100 squares. The red squares show the share of the 52 deaths. Most were men, almost half were aged 55 or over, and nearly two in three drowned more than 1 km from the nearest Surf Life Saving service.

Busy beaches are not the riskiest

Circle size shows how many people drowned in each Surf Life Saving NSW branch over 10 years. Circle colour shows the rate per 100,000 people.

Sydney has the most deaths (101) but one of the lower rates. The Far South Coast has fewer deaths but the highest rate, 4.55, about four times Sydney's.

Circles are placed at a main town in each branch, so their positions are approximate.

The people between the flags

More than 21,000 patrolling members kept watch on NSW beaches in 2024/25. They gave over 680,000 volunteer patrol hours.

3,494

rescues on NSW beaches in 2024/25

Each rectangle's size shows how many rescues were made that way. More than four in ten were made on a rescue board, the long foam board lifesavers paddle out to swimmers.

IRB means inflatable rescue boat and RWC means rescue watercraft (jet ski).
